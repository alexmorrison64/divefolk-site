Where to dive near DC · Virginia ocean wrecks

Wreck diving off Virginia Beach

Short answer: Virginia Beach is boat diving in the Atlantic, from a 45-foot light tower that classes use to World War II wrecks past 100 feet. Visibility can change from great to poor overnight, so it suits divers with some ocean experience. It's about 3½ hours from DC.

At a glance

  • From DC: about 207 miles to Lynnhaven Dive Center, roughly 3¼ hours by car from the White House with no traffic. Hampton Roads tunnel traffic can add a lot, so leave early.
  • How you dive: by charter boat only. The sites are miles offshore: the Chesapeake Light Tower is about 13 miles out, and the deeper wrecks are 20 to nearly 40 nautical miles.
  • Depths: about 45 feet at the tower, 60 to 85 feet on the nearer wrecks, and 100 to 120 feet on the bigger ones. Deeper wrecks are for technical divers.
  • Who it suits: divers comfortable in the ocean with currents, waves and changing visibility. Local divers describe boat diving here as best for advanced divers, though classes do use the light tower.

The sites, from shallow to deep

Depths below are from DiveVB, a local divers' site, unless noted. Wrecks change over time, so ask your captain about the current state of each one.

  • Chesapeake Light Tower, about 45 feet. An old offshore lighthouse on four big legs at the mouth of the Chesapeake Bay. The legs are covered in life and draw fish. A reviewer called it a very easy site that's good for practicing navigation.
  • Santore and Tiger, about 60 feet. Ships lost in 1942: the Santore to a mine laid by a German U-boat, the Tiger to a torpedo. Both are now mostly debris, and the Tiger, closer to shore, can have lower visibility.
  • Tower Tug (James B. Eskridge), about 60 feet. A 100-foot tug sunk as an artificial reef about 20 nautical miles out, with landing craft, barges and subway cars nearby, says Kraken Charters.
  • Gulf Hustler, about 70 to 85 feet. A clam boat that sank in 1974 and is still largely upright and intact. A popular trip with good structure for photos.
  • Kurn, about 100 feet. Two pieces about 50 yards apart, joined by a cable you can follow. Lots of sea bass.
  • John Morgan and Lillian Luckenbach, about 105 feet. Two World War II cargo ships lost in 1943 collisions. The John Morgan has a big debris field and hit-or-miss visibility; DiveVB still calls it highly recommended.
  • Cuyahoga, about 100 to 120 feet. A 125-foot Coast Guard cutter that sank after a collision in 1978 and was later sunk as a reef. It sits upright and intact.

Further out and deeper (170 to 250 feet) are technical dives such as the Ocean Venture and the German submarine U-117. Those need technical training.

What divers say about conditions

No one publishes official numbers, so these come from divers' reports. Treat them as rough.

  • Visibility swings a lot. A ScubaBoard member said one day on the offshore wrecks can be 60 feet and the next 5 feet. Near the light tower, water flowing out of the Chesapeake Bay can cut it to around 10 feet.
  • It's colder at depth. A Scuba Diving magazine writer found about 70°F at the surface dropping to the low 60s at 50 feet, with 30 feet of visibility once below the murky top layer.
  • Expect some current, and moving sand that can cut visibility, according to Virginia Beach's tourism site.
  • Season: charters run mostly in the warmer months. Check each operator's schedule before you plan a trip.

Who runs dive trips

  • Lynnhaven Dive Center, 1413 N Great Neck Road, Virginia Beach, (757) 481-7949, ldcscuba.com. A family-run dive shop since 1977 with its own charter boat, the Miss Mackenzie. Its schedule has included the Gulf Hustler, Brass Spike, the tug, the tower, the Santore and night dives. The shop fills air and nitrox.
  • Kraken Charters, call or text (757) 354-4606, krakenchartersva.com. A veteran-run charter boat that added scuba trips in 2025. Four divers at most. To book you need your certification, a logbook, DAN insurance, a buddy and a surface marker buoy with a spool. It's customary to tip the divemaster 20%.
  • Capt. JT's wreck site, capt-jt.com, describes wrecks off Virginia Beach and Hatteras and posts a dive boat schedule.

DiveFolk has no deals with any of these. Prices weren't posted online when we checked, so call.

Good to know for newer divers

  • Start at the light tower. It's the shallowest site, and classes dive it. Save the 100-foot wrecks for when you have deep training and some ocean dives behind you.
  • Bring a surface marker buoy and know how to send it up. At least one charter requires one, and on an offshore wreck in current it's how the boat finds you.
  • Get dive insurance. Kraken requires DAN insurance; it's smart for any offshore diving. See New to diving for DAN membership vs insurance.
  • Plan for the cold layer and the ride. Dress for the low 60s at depth, not the warm surface; see our wetsuit guide. The farther wrecks are a long ride offshore, so if you get seasick, ask your doctor about medicine before the trip.
  • Don't plan to fly the same day. Follow the flying-after-diving guidance on New to diving.
