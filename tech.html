Dive computer basics

Your dive computer tracks your depth and time and uses a decompression model to estimate how much nitrogen your body has taken on. From that, it shows a few numbers that matter most:

  • No-decompression limit (NDL). How many more minutes you can stay at your current depth and still ascend directly to the surface, with a safety stop.
  • Ascent rate. Most computers warn you if you come up too fast. Slow ascents are one of the simplest safety habits.
  • Safety stop. Many computers count down a stop near 15 feet at the end of a dive.
  • Gas. If you dive nitrox, your computer needs the right oxygen percentage set before the dive.
  • Surface interval and no-fly time. After a dive, many computers show time since surfacing and how long to wait before flying.

Every computer is different. Read your manual, and practice with your settings on easy dives first.

Gradient factors, explained

Many computers, including Shearwater and Garmin dive computers, use a decompression model called Bühlmann. Gradient factors are two percentages that make that model more or less conservative.

  • GF high sets how close to the model's limit you can be when you reach the surface. For most recreational diving, this is the number that matters most. A lower GF high means shorter no-deco times and more margin.
  • GF low mainly shapes deeper stops on dives that need decompression, so it rarely affects typical recreational dives.
  • Settings like "low, medium, high" conservatism are usually preset gradient factor pairs.

No setting guarantees safety; decompression sickness can happen inside a computer's limits. If you're unsure which setting to use, choose the more conservative one and ask your instructor.

Source: DAN Alert Diver, "Gradient Factors"

Air integration

With air integration, a small transmitter screws into your regulator's first stage and sends your tank pressure wirelessly to your dive computer. The computer can then show your pressure, your gas use and an estimate of time remaining.

  • The transmitter has to be paired with your computer and has its own battery.
  • Signals can drop out briefly, especially when another diver's transmitter is close by.
  • Many divers also keep an analog pressure gauge as a backup.

Your dive data files

Dive computers and apps save your dives in different file types. Knowing which one you have makes it easier to move your log where you want it.

Back up your dive log

A dive log is hard to rebuild if you lose it. A simple routine keeps it safe:

  • After each trip, export your dives from your dive computer's app (UDDF and CSV are good choices).
  • Keep everything in one folder you own: your spreadsheet, the computer files and photos of your paper logbook pages.
  • Keep a second copy somewhere else, such as a cloud drive or an external drive.
  • An app that syncs to the cloud is convenient, but it's not the same as a copy you control.

Are your dive times off by an hour?

Most dive computers keep whatever clock time you set. Fly somewhere in a different time zone without changing it, and every dive on the trip is logged an hour or more off. Your paper log and your computer won't agree, and dives can land on the wrong day.

  • Before a trip: set your computer's clock to local time when you arrive. Watches that sync with your phone may do this for you, but check.
  • After a trip: the DiveFolk Logbook has a Clock fix for each dive, and if you add your paper log it tells you when the times disagree and by how much.
